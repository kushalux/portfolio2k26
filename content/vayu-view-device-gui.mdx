Most of Vayu View lives on a screen someone chooses to open. The air quality monitor doesn't. It hangs on a wall in a lobby or an office floor, and it is read by people who never log in, never received training, and are giving it about two seconds of attention as they walk past.

That makes it a completely different design problem from the [dashboard](/case-studies/vayu-view-dashboard) — same data, opposite constraints.

## The audience is different

The dashboard has one user: the Building Administration Head, who is accountable for the space and wants detail.

The device has two, and they want opposite things:

- **The occupant** — a tenant, an employee, a hotel guest. Not accountable for anything. Wants one question answered: *is the air in this room okay?* They will not tap anything, and they will not stand still.
- **The administrator** — the same person who uses the dashboard, but now standing physically in front of the unit. Wants confirmation that the device is alive and reporting sensible values.

Designing for the first without failing the second is the whole job.

## The constraints are real

A dashboard can assume a good screen, a mouse, and an attentive user sitting a foot away. The monitor can assume none of that. It has a fixed physical screen, it's read from across a room rather than at arm's length, and it is often in bad lighting.

Those constraints do most of the design work for you if you let them. They rule out small type, dense layouts, subtle colour differences, and anything that requires interaction to reveal meaning.

## The two-second problem

An occupant walking past gives the display roughly two seconds. In that time they need to leave with one of two conclusions: *the air is fine* or *the air is not fine*.

That means the primary state cannot be a number. "PM2.5: 38 µg/m³" is meaningless to someone with no reference for what 38 means or whether it's good. The number is only useful to someone who already knows the scale — which is the administrator, not the occupant.

So the display has to carry a verdict first and evidence second: state the conclusion in a form that reads instantly, and let the underlying values support it for anyone who cares to look longer.

## Why this one matters

Very few product designers have shipped an interface that runs on hardware. The constraints force a kind of discipline that doesn't come up in web work: you cannot add a tooltip, you cannot open a modal, you cannot ask the user to scroll. Everything has to be resolved in the composition itself.
